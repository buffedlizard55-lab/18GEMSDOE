01 · H18-N1 — Paleolake shoreline displacement

Physical mechanism

Fault slip across a former lake basin may vertically offset or warp shoreline benches and post-lake surfaces. The evidential object is repeated, same-sense displacement on at least two independently correlated marker levels across a narrow zone—not an edge that merely resembles a fault scarp. The output should trace the marker discontinuity, not the shoreline itself.

Why it could reveal unmapped strands

Faulting can occur in basin-fill or lacustrine deposits away from a mapped range front. Independent marker surfaces offer a datum that may expose an extension, splay, or parallel strand inside an existing fault zone. DrivenData staff confirm these geometries may count as new labeled pixels, but have not disclosed their hidden-label source methods.

Non-fault alternatives and controls

Wave-cut erosional scarps, beach ridges, differential compaction, broad isostatic tilt, fluvial incision, fan-margin erosion, canals, road/rail embankments, and DEM mosaic seams can all create linear relief. Require multiple correlated levels with consistent displacement sense; model/remove broad regional tilt; reject infrastructure-aligned breaks; compare shoreline-only, channel, road, and seam placebo controls. A broad smooth tilt without a localized discontinuity falsifies the specific fault mechanism.

Data and discriminating test

USGS MF-2323 publicly lists paleolake GIS, but its shoreline contours were interpreted from roughly 90 m DEMs—context, not 100 m-scale offset evidence. Public-domain 3DEP 1 m DEM coverage, acquisition seams, and GeoDAWN overlap remain unchecked. Before tuning, hold out complete basins/fault systems, not random pixels. Score against independently mapped traces withheld from the detector, include shoreline-only scarps and human-made/channel controls, and use a basin-level block bootstrap. Require improvement over a reproduced comparator at both dense and sparse conditions. If fine-scale DEM coverage or independent marker correlation fails, stop.

Expected value/cost: ranked first because independent, same-sense marker offsets are comparatively diagnostic and extension/splay-focused. Useful coverage may be narrow; this is a source-audit priority, not validated expected gain.

02 · H18-N2 — Focal-mechanism plane projection

Physical mechanism

Several independent earthquakes with reviewed moment tensors, compatible nodal-plane orientations, and a coherent hypocenter trend can identify a subsurface brittle shear plane. Project the plane and its dip/location uncertainty toward the surface. Do not turn event density alone into a fault line, emit a long trace from one solution, or choose the favorable nodal plane after seeing the holdout.

Why it could reveal unmapped strands

An active uncatalogued strand or continuation may have a coherent subsurface rupture plane even when its surface trace is absent from a hazard catalog. It could target allowed extensions or splays, but misses inactive or locked faults.

Non-fault alternatives and controls

Volcanic or fluid-driven swarms, mining blasts, unrelated faults in one swarm, poor hypocenter locations, and nodal-plane ambiguity can mimic a plane. Use reviewed double-couple solutions with uncertainty; require agreement across independent events; perturb both nodal planes and location/dip uncertainty; compare matched random mechanisms and time-shuffled events. Reject unstable projections.

Data and discriminating test

USGS ComCat documentation provides source parameters and moment-tensor products, but event count and solution quality in the scored footprint have not been audited. Leave entire earthquake sequences and fault systems out. Catalog traces may be held-out truth, never model input. Compare with matched non-fault seismic clusters and use block-level uncertainty; drop this candidate quickly if there are too few independent mechanisms or its result depends on one aftershock family.

Expected value/cost: a credible fault-mechanical signal, but likely sparse and recall-limited; high curation and validation cost.

03 · H18-N3 — Sentinel-1 InSAR displacement discontinuity

Physical mechanism

A creeping or coseismically slipping fault may create a spatially coherent displacement gradient or step in repeat-pass radar line-of-sight time series. Emit only a narrow corridor supported by a persistent fault-consistent signal, not one interferogram fringe. This is a temporal deformation measurement, not another static strain or lineament index.

Why it could reveal unmapped strands

A presently deforming but uncatalogued strand could extend a mapped trace or form a splay. Locked and inactive faults are invisible to this detector, so it cannot be generalized to a complete fault inventory.

Non-fault alternatives and controls

Groundwater extraction and aquifer compaction, seasonal soil moisture or thermal expansion, atmospheric delay, ionospheric artifacts, topographic phase error, vegetation decorrelation, and anthropogenic subsidence all create apparent gradients. Require persistence over independent windows, compare ascending/descending viewing geometries where available, mask incoherent pixels, remove basin-wide seasonal and known subsidence signals, and test groundwater-change basins as negative controls. Signals that track subsidence bowls, reverse seasonally, or disappear across tracks are not fault detections.

Data and discriminating test

NASA ASF DAAC documents Sentinel-1 access through Vertex and Earthdata; download/tool access may require an Earthdata account. Exact GeoDAWN time coverage and coherence have not been queried. Leave whole active fault systems and time windows out; evaluate only against independently supported recent movement and compare to groundwater-subsidence controls. Proceed only if a coverage audit finds enough coherent scenes and independent faults for a blocked test.

Expected value/cost: potentially precise but low coverage, artifact-heavy, and very costly; last-ranked.

Scarp-diffusion science: caution, not a fourth hypothesis

Hanks et al. (1984) and Andrews & Hanks (1985) are primary work on linear-diffusion scarp morphology. Hilley et al. (2010) fit a second-derivative curvature template in high-resolution topography to identify scarp-like forms. The inversion commonly constrains diffusivity × time, κt, not calendar age without an independent diffusivity calibration. Hanks et al. analyze wave-cut/shoreline scarps as well as fault scarps. Sare et al. (2019) report nontectonic scarp-like candidates in regional template results, including roads and channels; visual review was used to remove false positives. The Dixie Valley GBCGE case documents a scarp later found by trenching to be erosional. Shape or apparent age alone is not tectonic evidence. See the verified primary sources in the source registry.

Decision: begin only with a source/coverage audit for H18-N1. Freeze the spatial holdout before implementation. If authorized data, independent labels, high-resolution coverage, or split provenance cannot be established, stop rather than tune on an invalid proxy.
